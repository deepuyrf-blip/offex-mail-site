Custom domain email vs temp mail for side projects

Updated for 2026 · 8 min read

If you run a side project, you have probably faced the same choice twice: what address should the project use, and what address should you use to sign up for other people’s tools? The two answers are usually different, and reaching for the same tool for both is where people get into trouble.

Two different jobs

A custom domain address is an identity: something you own, keep and can move between providers. A disposable inbox is a door: something you use once and discard. Confusing the two leads either to paying for infrastructure you do not need, or to losing access to accounts you do.

What a custom domain gives you

  • Ownership. The address belongs to a domain you control, so it survives changing email providers.
  • Consistency. Every message your project sends comes from the same recognisable domain, which builds trust.
  • Deliverability. A clean, purpose-built domain is less likely to be filtered than a free address.
  • Unlimited aliases. You can give each service its own address on your domain and route them as you like.

What it does not give you is anonymity, and it is not free — you pay for the domain, and usually for hosting or forwarding.

What a temp inbox gives you

A disposable address gives you the opposite trade. It costs nothing, needs no setup, asks nothing about you, and disappears on its own. That makes it perfect for sampling tools, testing flows and clearing one-off gates — and useless for anything you must keep. You cannot build an identity on an address that is designed to expire.

The cost and maintenance of a custom domain

A domain is cheap but not free, and it comes with upkeep: renewals, DNS records, and deliverability settings if you send mail from it. For a project that emails users, that upkeep is worth it. For a weekend experiment, it is overhead you will resent. The question is not “which is better” but “does this project send mail people rely on?”

A sensible split for a solo project

A workable pattern for one person running a few things:

  1. Send from a custom domain. Your project’s outbound mail — confirmations, notifications — should come from a domain you own, so it is trustworthy and portable.
  2. Sign up with disposable addresses. Every third-party tool you try gets a throwaway, so your project domain stays out of their marketing lists.
  3. Keep one permanent personal address for accounts you pay for or must recover.
  4. Add a project alias on your domain for the handful of services you keep long-term.

Run that way, each address does the job it is good at. The domain carries your identity outward; the disposable inbox absorbs everything you are only sampling; and your personal address stays reserved for what matters. The mistake to avoid is trying to make one address do all three — that is how people end up either paying for anonymity they do not need or losing a project login they cannot recover.

A note on sending mail from a temp address

One limitation is easy to overlook: a disposable inbox is built for receiving, which is what sign-ups and confirmations need. It is not the tool you send from. If your project needs to reply to users, send notifications or handle support, that mail has to come from an address you own and can keep. This is the clearest dividing line between the two tools. Receiving is a door you can throw away; sending is a voice that has to belong to someone permanent. Decide which one the task needs before you pick an address, and the choice is usually obvious.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use a custom domain or temp mail for a project?

Use a custom domain for anything your project sends or that you must keep, and a disposable inbox for the third-party tools you are only trying.

Is a custom domain worth the cost?

If your project emails users and you want a trustworthy, portable identity, yes. For a short experiment, a disposable address is enough.

Can I mix both?

Yes, and you probably should: a custom domain for identity, disposable inboxes for sampling tools, and one permanent address for accounts you pay for.

Do I need a custom domain to look professional?

For anything user-facing, a domain you own reads as more trustworthy and travels better than a free address. For internal testing, it is unnecessary.
