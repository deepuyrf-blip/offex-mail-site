How to spot a phishing email in any inbox

Updated for 2026 · 7 min read

Phishing is the most common way accounts are stolen, and it rarely involves clever software. It involves a convincing email and a moment of haste. The good news is that phishing is also one of the most pattern-heavy crimes there is: once you know the tells, most attempts look obvious.

The four tells that matter most

  1. The sender does not match the claim. A message that says it is from a bank but comes from an unrelated address is a red flag, no matter how polished the design.
  2. The link goes somewhere unexpected. The visible text may read like a familiar domain, but the actual destination can differ. Check before you click.
  3. The message creates urgency. “Your account will be closed in 24 hours” is engineered to stop you thinking. Legitimate services rarely threaten you into a same-day click.
  4. It asks for something no real service asks for by email. Passwords, full card numbers and one-time codes are things a genuine sender does not request by reply.

Why a temp inbox sees more phishing

Disposable addresses are collected aggressively, precisely because they are cheap and abundant. That means a fresh temp inbox can start receiving junk within minutes, and some of it will be phishing aimed at whatever the sender thinks the address is worth. Seeing it is not a problem; the problem is only clicking it.

In fact, a throwaway inbox is a good place to learn what phishing looks like, because the stakes are low. Nothing valuable is tied to the address.

Reading the sender, not the display name

The name shown in a mail client is just a label the sender chose; it proves nothing. The real identity is the address and, behind it, the domain. A message claiming to be from a well-known brand should come from that brand’s own domain, not from a lookalike with an extra hyphen or a different ending. When a verification email arrives right after you signed up somewhere, compare the sender against what you expect — a mismatch is your cue to slow down.

What to do when you are unsure

  • Do not click. Open a new tab and go to the service by typing its address yourself.
  • Hover, do not tap. On desktop, rest the cursor on a link to preview its destination before committing.
  • Check the request against reality. If you did not request a password reset or a new sign-in, treat the mail as suspect by default.
  • Never reply with a code. A one-time code is the one thing an attacker most wants, and no legitimate support agent needs it.

The one rule that defeats almost every phishing email

If you remember nothing else: never act on a link or a code from an email you did not expect. Go to the site directly and do whatever the message claims to need there. That single habit removes the attacker’s entire method, because phishing depends on you arriving at their page through their link, not through the real site.

Used with a disposable inbox, that rule makes the low-stakes sign-ups you do all week genuinely safe: you get the confirmation you need, and everything else in the inbox is just noise you are free to ignore.

Why “I have nothing to lose” is a trap

People often assume a throwaway inbox makes them safe from phishing, and that is only half true. The inbox is disposable; the device you open it on is not, and the passwords you type anywhere are not. A phishing page does not care which inbox you came from — it cares what you type into it. So the discipline travels with you: the same caution you would apply to your real mail applies to a burner inbox, because the target is you, not the mailbox.

A quick habit for the whole week

You do not need to become a security expert. A single default covers most situations: whenever a form asks for an email and you did not go looking for the service, treat the address as untrusted and reach any follow-up directly rather than through the message. That one habit, applied without effort, neutralises the vast majority of phishing because it removes the click the attacker depends on. Everything else in this guide is refinement.

Frequently asked questions

Are phishing emails common in temporary inboxes?

Yes. Disposable addresses are collected aggressively, so a fresh inbox can receive junk and phishing quickly. Because nothing valuable is tied to the address, they are low-risk to observe and easy to ignore.

Can clicking a phishing link be dangerous even in a throwaway inbox?

The inbox is disposable but your device and your real accounts are not. A phishing page can still try to install something or harvest credentials you type, so never click links you did not expect.

How do I check a link before clicking it?

On a computer, hover over the link and read the real destination. On a phone, long-press to preview it. If the destination does not match the sender's real domain, do not open it.

Does Offex Mail filter phishing for me?

Treat every inbox as unfiltered and apply your own judgement. The safe habit is to reach sites directly rather than through links in unexpected mail.
