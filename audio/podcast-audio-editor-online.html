What "editing a podcast" actually involves

New podcasters imagine editing is about creative decisions: what to cut, where to add music, how to shape the story. In practice, most of the time goes into four repetitive chores that have nothing to do with creativity:

  1. Trimming dead air. Every episode is padded with silence — before you start, between sentences, while you think, and after you finish.
  2. Reducing background noise. Room tone, a fan, a computer, a phone buzzing on the desk.
  3. Balancing two voices. Guests and hosts rarely record at the same level, and one is often further from the mic.
  4. Exporting the right format. Bitrate, sample rate and channel count decide how the episode sounds once it reaches a listener.

Three of those four can be handled free, in the browser. That is the part this site is built for.

Step 1 — Remove the silence

The Remove Silence tool trims the pauses that make an episode feel slow. You upload (locally — the file is not sent anywhere), set how much silence to keep, and process. Keeping a short pause of about a quarter of a second at each cut preserves the rhythm of conversation, so the episode sounds tighter without sounding choppy. This one change is usually the biggest perceived quality improvement for a new show.

Step 2 — Reduce the background noise

Next, run the episode through the Enhance Audio tool. It applies a gentle high-pass filter and an adaptive noise gate, then normalises the level. Use Light if the recording is already quiet, Balanced for most rooms, and Strong only for genuinely noisy sources. If the voices start to sound watery, you have gone too far — step back a mode.

Step 3 — Balance the voices

Matching loudness between speakers is fiddly by hand, but the principle is simple: clean each voice, then bring both to the same average loudness before you compress anything. If you are editing a two-track recording, do this per track. If it is a single mixed track, you will have less control — which is one good reason to record separate tracks when you can.

Step 4 — Export in the right format

For a finished spoken-word episode, a mono MP3 at 96 to 128 kbps is more than enough and keeps the file small. Music-heavy shows benefit from 160 to 192 kbps. Keep a lossless master (WAV) and export the compressed MP3 once at the end rather than repeatedly, because every re-encode loses a little quality. The podcast format guide goes into bitrate, sample rate and channels in more detail.

Where a browser editor stops

Be realistic about the limits. Free online tools are excellent for cleanup — silence, noise, level and format. They are not a full multi-track workstation. If your episode needs a music bed with ducking, several overlapping clips, detailed EQ per voice, or broadcast-standard loudness metering, you will still want a desktop editor for that final polish. The smart workflow is to do the repetitive cleanup online first, export a clean master, and open the heavier tool only for the creative parts.

Frequently asked questions

Can I edit a podcast entirely online for free?

For the cleanup stage, yes — removing silence and reducing noise both run free in your browser. Final music, multi-track mixing and detailed mastering are still easier in a desktop editor.

What is the first thing to edit in a podcast episode?

Remove the dead air. Cutting silence first makes every later step faster and makes the conversation tighter, which is the single biggest quality win for most shows.

Will online editing reduce my audio quality?

Only if you re-encode repeatedly. Export once at the end, keep a lossless master, and choose WAV for the final cleanup step if your host supports it.
