Why Is My Recording Noisy?

Before you can remove noise, you have to know which noise you have. This guide is a diagnostic: it teaches you to recognise the common types of noise by ear, find their real cause, and decide whether to fix the source or the file.

"Noisy" is not one problem; it is several, and they sound different. Learning to tell them apart is the single most useful audio skill there is, because each type has a different cause and a different fix. If you skip this step and just run a generic denoiser, you will often make the wrong thing quieter and leave the real problem in place.

Hiss — the steady "sssss"

Hiss is a constant, even shhhh sound, like a radio tuned to nothing. It comes from the electronics in your signal chain: a noisy preamp, a cheap microphone, or simply a gain turned up too high. It is worst when you boost a quiet signal a long way. The fix: record louder at the source so you need less gain later, use a cleaner input, and only then apply gentle noise reduction. Because hiss is constant, a denoiser handles it well — but do not overdo it, or the voice goes watery.

Hum — the low "mmmmm"

Hum is a low, steady tone, usually at the mains frequency of your country — 50 Hz in much of the world, 60 Hz in North America. It comes from electrical interference: a ground loop, a bad cable, a charger or a power supply sitting near the microphone or the cable. The fix: move the microphone and cable away from power supplies and chargers, replace a suspect cable, and check for a ground loop. In the edit, a narrow notch filter at the exact hum frequency removes it almost completely without harming the voice.

Buzz and crackle — the rough ones

Buzz is like hum but harsher and richer, often a "bzzzz" with harmonics; crackle is a random, irregular popping. Buzz usually means a dirty connection or a device charging nearby. Crackle often means a failing cable, a loose connector, or a bad jack. The fix: these are physical problems, so fix the hardware — reseat or replace the cable and move away from chargers. A denoiser handles crackle poorly because it is irregular, so prevention matters more than ever.

Rumble — the very low one

Rumble is low-frequency energy you can feel more than hear: footsteps, traffic, a hand bumping the desk, a nearby road, or the building's own vibration. The fix: use a shock mount or a soft surface under the microphone, avoid touching the desk while recording, and apply a high-pass filter around 80–100 Hz. Rumble is easy to remove because voices carry almost nothing that low.

Room tone — the honest one

Room tone is the quiet, shapeless sound of the space itself: a faint hum from the building, a distant fan, the low murmur of a city. Every room has it, and it is not a fault — but if it is loud, it makes the voice sound distant and unprofessional. The fix: reduce it at the source (fans, windows, fridges) and trim the silence so it is less exposed. A gentle noise reduction can lower it further.

Digital noise — the fixable one

Sometimes the noise is not the recording at all but the way it was processed. Heavy compression in a call app, aggressive automatic noise reduction, or a low-bitrate export can add a thin, swishy, robotic quality. The fix: go back to the original, unprocessed file, or change the settings in the app that introduced the artefact.

How to diagnose quickly

  1. Listen on headphones, with the loudness turned up, during a gap where nobody is speaking.
  2. Ask: is it steady (hiss, hum, room tone) or irregular (crackle, bumps)? Steady noise is easy to remove; irregular noise is usually a physical fault.
  3. Is it a tone (hum, buzz) or a wash (hiss, room tone)? A tone wants a notch; a wash wants a denoiser.
  4. Is it low (rumble, hum) or spread across the range (hiss)? Low problems want a high-pass filter.

Fix the source, then the file

Whatever the noise, the order is always the same: remove it at the source first, then clean the file. A recording made with the charger unplugged and the fan off needs almost no processing, while one made next to a power supply may never be fully clean. When you do process, do it gently and in the right order: trim, then denoise, then filter the low end, then normalise. Conclusion

Noisy recordings are a set of specific, diagnosable problems, not a mystery. Learn the sound of hiss, hum, buzz, crackle, rumble and room tone, find the cause, and fix it at the source where you can. Then apply the right edit — a notch for a tone, a denoiser for a wash, a high-pass for the low end — gently and in order. Diagnose first, and every recording gets easier to clean.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my recording so noisy?

Usually one of a few causes: electronics hiss from too much gain, mains hum from a cable or charger, rumble from vibration, or plain room tone. Each has a different fix.

How do I tell hum from hiss?

Hum is a low steady tone, like a quiet 'mmmm'. Hiss is a higher steady wash, like 'sssss'. Hum wants a notch filter; hiss wants a gentle denoiser.

Can I remove crackling from a recording?

Crackle is irregular and hard to remove in software because it looks like wanted sound. It is usually a failing cable or connector, so fix the hardware rather than the file.

What is room tone and is it a problem?

Room tone is the quiet sound of the space itself. Every room has it and it is not a fault, but a loud room tone makes the voice sound distant, so reduce it at the source.
