How to Remove Echo and Reverb From a Voice Recording

Offex Audio · Practical audio-editing guide

Echo and reverb are the two most common complaints about home-recorded voice. They sound similar but come from different causes, and treating them well depends on knowing which one you have. Reverb is the dense, short wash of reflections that a room adds to every syllable — it makes a voice sound distant and boxy. Echo is a distinct, repeatable repetition, often a single slap or a flutter, that arrives clearly after the original sound. This guide explains how to identify each, what to fix before you touch any software, and how to reduce both with the tools built into almost every audio editor, plus what to do when the problem is severe.

First, Diagnose What You Actually Have

Put on headphones and listen to a single word in isolation. Loop it.

  • If the word smears into a tail that decays smoothly and disappears within half a second, you have reverb. This is a room problem.
  • If you hear the word repeat once clearly, or bounce back and forth in a metallic flutter, you have echo. This is usually a reflection from one hard surface, a delay from a digital connection, or a mismatch between two recordings.

The distinction matters because the fixes differ. Reverb responds to room treatment and to a de-reverb or gate approach. Echo often responds to removing the specific delay or, in bad cases, to re-recording. Trying to fix echo with a de-reverb tool is frustrating because the tool hears the echo as part of the signal.

Why This Happens

Every hard, flat surface reflects sound. Your voice leaves your mouth, hits a wall or window, and bounces back to the microphone a few milliseconds later. In a small untreated room, you get dozens of these reflections arriving so close together they blend into one blur — reverb. A single dominant reflection, like a bare wall directly behind the microphone, produces a discrete echo.

Digital echo has different causes. A monitoring path that routes your output back into the input, a Bluetooth or conferencing connection with latency, or a second microphone left open in the same room can all create a clean, repeatable delay that sounds like an echo.

What to Fix Before Software

No plugin removes reverb as cleanly as simply not recording it. If you still have the option to re-record, spend ten minutes improving the space instead of an hour fighting the recording.

  1. Move away from bare parallel walls. Sit at an angle to the room so reflections do not bounce straight back at you.
  2. Put something soft behind the microphone. A blanket, a coat, a cushion — anything to catch the first reflection.
  3. Get closer to the mic. Doubling your proximity to the microphone roughly doubles the direct sound relative to the room, which is the same as turning the reverb down.
  4. Close the window and pull the curtains. Glass is a near-perfect reflector and one of the worst offenders.
  5. For digital echo, check your routing. Confirm you are monitoring only the input, not the output, and that no second device is open.

If the recording is already made, the next sections are your toolkit.

Reducing Reverb With a Noise Gate

A gate silences the signal when it drops below a threshold. Because reverb tails are quieter than the words themselves, a well-set gate trims the tail during the gaps between words, which makes speech sound tighter and more present.

Set the threshold so it sits just above the reverb tail but below the quietest parts of your actual speech. If you set it too high, the gate chops off the ends of words and you hear an unnatural "clipping off" at the end of every phrase. A gentle approach beats an aggressive one: a moderate threshold, a fast but not instant attack (around 5 to 10 milliseconds), a short hold, and a release of 50 to 150 milliseconds so the gate closes smoothly rather than snapping shut.

A gate does not remove reverb from under the words — it only cleans the gaps. Used carefully, it makes a big difference in perceived clarity. Used heavily, it makes speech sound robotic.

Expansion Instead of Gating

If a hard gate sounds too choppy, try a downward expander, sometimes labelled "expander" or "gentle gate." It reduces the level of quiet passages gradually rather than muting them outright. This preserves the natural feel of the voice while still pushing the reverb tail down. Many editors bundle an expander or a "noise gate" with expander behaviour. The expander is the better default for voice.

De-Reverb and Spectral Approaches

Some editors and plugins include a dedicated de-reverb or "dereverb" processor. These analyse the decay and try to subtract the tail. They work best on mild to moderate reverb, and they degrade quickly on heavy reverb — overused, they introduce a watery, metallic artefact that sounds worse than the original problem. Use them lightly, compare before and after, and stop as soon as the voice starts sounding hollow.

A related technique is spectral editing. In a spectral view, reverb appears as faint energy spreading after each word. You can select and reduce those regions by hand. This is slow and detailed work, best reserved for short, important passages like an audiobook chapter or a single ad read.

The Two-Microphone Trick

If you have two recordings of the same voice — for example, a close mic and a room mic — you can phase-invert one against the other to cancel the shared reverb. This is advanced and rarely available to home recorders, but it is worth knowing that echo cancellation in video calls uses the same principle: it subtracts a delayed copy of a known signal.

Removing Discrete Echo

For a clear single echo, a targeted approach works better than a broad de-reverb.

  1. Measure the delay. Zoom into the waveform, find the original peak of a sharp word, and find its echo. The distance between them is your delay time, often 20 to 80 milliseconds for a wall reflection.
  2. Use a short, high-pass filtered delay set to that exact time, mixed in at low level with the polarity flipped, to partially cancel the echo. This is finicky and imperfect, but on a clean, isolated echo it can help.
  3. Alternatively, gate the signal tightly so the echo, which arrives during a gap, is muted.

If the echo is from a second open microphone or a conferencing artefact, the honest answer is that the cleanest fix is re-recording that segment. Software can mask a little echo; it cannot convincingly rebuild a clean voice from a badly echoed one.

Order of Operations

Processing order affects the result, so follow a sensible sequence:

  1. Trim and remove silence so you are working on speech only.
  2. Reduce steady background noise before touching reverb, so the de-reverb does not try to subtract noise.
  3. Apply gentle expansion or gating to tighten the gaps.
  4. Use a light de-reverb if the editor has one, checking constantly.
  5. Add a subtle high-pass filter around 80 to 100 Hz to remove low rumble that muddies the voice.
  6. Finally, apply compression and level matching.

This order prevents one process from amplifying another's artefacts. Doing noise reduction last, for example, would lift the reverb you just tried to reduce.

A Note on Over-Processing

It is easy to keep stacking fixes until the voice sounds artificial. Reverb removal is a trade-off between clarity and naturalness. A small amount of natural room sound is not a flaw — it is what makes a voice sound human. Aim for "tighter and clearer," not "sterile." Compare against the original with matched loudness; louder almost always sounds "better" even when it is worse.

Conclusion

Echo and reverb are room and routing problems first, and editing problems second. Diagnose which one you have, fix the space or the signal path if you still can, then treat the recording with gentle tools in the right order: trim silence, reduce noise, tighten gaps with expansion or a soft gate, apply light de-reverb, filter low rumble, and level. Avoid aggressive settings that trade naturalness for a hollow, metallic voice. When the reverb is heavy, re-recording in a softer, closer position will always beat trying to erase it. With a sensible chain and a little patience, even a reflective room can produce a voice that sounds close, clear, and professional.
